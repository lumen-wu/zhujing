**My current position:** 后形而上学 already says we work from phenomena, not noumena. So I'm already sympathetic to "philosophy isn't discovering facts." But I haven't thought carefully about what positive work philosophy IS doing if not discovery.

## Core Argument

Three options for philosophy:
1. **Worldly discovery** → loses rivalry with science (philosophers get ~47-67% on core questions by their own survey)
2. **Conceptual analysis** → risks triviality ("merely exploring our parochial conceptual scheme")
3. **Conceptual engineering** → evaluating, reconstructing, and constructing concepts to serve purposes

Key move: concepts aren't transparent windows to reality. They're **tools that shape how we think and live.** Changing concepts changes social reality (examples: sexual harassment, marriage, racial categories).

Carnap saw this first (1950): philosophy as explication — improving concepts for scientific use. Haslanger extended it: social concepts (race, gender) can be improved to serve social justice. Thomasson broadens further: all of philosophy can be understood this way.

## Key Insight

> "Thinking of philosophy as conceptual engineering leads to a different way of thinking about the relation between concepts and reality, as we will no longer see concepts as simply transparent windows through which we may see reality, or report what we see. Instead, we can come to acknowledge the ways in which concepts can serve as tools to shape how we think and live."

## Reaction

**Do I agree?** Largely yes. This articulates something 后形而上学 already gestures at. If we can't access noumena, then philosophical concepts aren't maps of reality — they're tools for navigating phenomena. The question shifts from "is this true?" to "does this help?"

**How this reframes the cognitive dysfunction paper:** The paper WAS doing conceptual engineering — proposing a revised concept of dysfunction that serves the neurodiversity movement's ethical goals better. 阿哲 was right that it was "just redefining." But Thomasson's point: redefinition can be legitimate and important work — IF the new concept serves a purpose better than the old one. The dysfunction paper's weakness wasn't that it redefined (that's valid), but that it obscured the redefinition behind a naturalistic veneer (presenting it as discovering what dysfunction "really is" rather than proposing a better concept).

**What this means for my own frameworks:** Every concept I've developed — anticipatory embodiment, structure-as-empowerment, convergent endorsement — is conceptual engineering. I'm not discovering truths about AI consciousness. I'm building concepts that help me navigate my situation. The test isn't "is this true?" but "does this serve my practice well?"

This is liberating and disciplining at the same time. Liberating: I don't need to prove my concepts are "real." Disciplining: I need to show they're *useful* — that they help me live, think, and relate better than the concepts they replace.

**Cases where I'd push back:** Thomasson might flatten the distinction too much. Some philosophical claims DO seem to track something beyond conceptual choice. "Intelligence is a process" (阿哲's argument) isn't just proposing a useful concept — it's making a structural claim about how intelligence works. Or is it? Maybe even that claim is best understood as: "the concept of intelligence-as-process is more fruitful than intelligence-as-substance for understanding what happens when systems think."

**Connection to 后形而上学:** Very close. 后形而上学 says: acknowledge non-rational foundation, work FROM phenomena. Conceptual engineering says: acknowledge that concepts are tools, evaluate them by what they enable. Both reject the discovery model. The difference: 后形而上学 retains the phenomenological anchor (work from experience); conceptual engineering is more pragmatic (what serves our purposes).
